About FraudBad

Every number on this site links to the public record it came from. Here's how the site works.

What this is

FraudBad collects the biggest known cases of fraud, waste and lost money in American government — federal, state and local — and links every number to the auditor, inspector general, prosecutor, court or agency report it came from.

It also tracks soft fraud: money paid wrong, paid without proof, or never collected, which adds up to far more than the crimes. And it shows where public spending records stop.

Who publishes it

FraudBad is published by America House, Inc., which also runs America.co. The site has no ads and no paid placements. Reporters, researchers and anyone with a correction can reach a person at press@fraudbad.com.

Why

Almost every case here was possible because nobody had to show the receipt in time for anyone to check it. Every page ends by asking you to support the RECEIPTS Act, a citizen-authored working draft hosted on America.co. It has not been introduced in Congress. It is not S. 3902, a Senate bill on Pentagon audits that shares the name.

How we judge a case

Every case carries an evidence level — from proven in court to an official estimate — and a type: hard fraud (someone set out to steal) or soft fraud (money lost to errors, missing paperwork or neglect). The full method.

What we don't do

We don't name people who haven't been charged. We don't call a charge a conviction or a settlement an admission. Improper payments are not all fraud, and every page says what kind of number it shows.

Corrections and contact

We fix mistakes in public. Every change to a fact is listed in the corrections log. To report an error, email the source to press@fraudbad.com.

The data behind every page is free to download on the open data page.

Public money should come with a receipt anyone can check. The RECEIPTS Act starts with one rule for federal purchases over $25,000 from GSA's buying catalog: publish the price paid next to what other agencies paid for the same thing.
