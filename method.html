Method

How we judge

No invented numbers. No guesses dressed up as facts. Every figure on this site comes from an auditor, an inspector general, a prosecutor, a court or the agency that paid the money — and every page says which.

How a case makes the list

A case is listed when an official body has put a dollar figure on fraud, likely fraud, or payments nobody can prove were right, and the amount runs to hundreds of millions of dollars or more. That body can be the Government Accountability Office, an inspector general, a state auditor or comptroller, the Justice Department, a court, or the agency itself.

News reports are used to show what officials said and when. Wherever we could, we link to the official document itself.

Hard fraud and soft fraud

Hard fraud is theft and deception: schemes, crimes, and official estimates of fraud. Soft fraud is negligence: money paid without proof, paid too much, paid twice, lost track of, or never collected. Nobody has to break a law for soft fraud to happen, and nobody gets charged — which is why it's bigger. Here's exactly how we search for it.

How sure are we?

Every case carries one label. It tells you what kind of number you're looking at.

Confirmed

Proven in court, admitted in a guilty plea, or confirmed by the agency that paid it.

Charged

Prosecutors filed charges. These are accusations; everyone charged is presumed innocent until proven guilty.

Settled

Money was paid to settle the government's claims, without an admission of wrongdoing.

Estimated

An auditor, inspector general or the agency itself estimated the fraud. It's a measured range, not a list of names.

Paid without proof

Officially counted as paid in error or without the records to prove it was right. Some is fraud. Nobody has measured how much.

Payments frozen

Officials stopped payment over suspected fraud. No one has been found guilty.

Can't be checked

The books or the spending can't be verified, so fraud can't be ruled in or out.

Over budget

Officially measured cost growth or delay beyond what was promised.

Fix ignored

A watchdog found the waste and the fix. The fix hasn't been made.

Measured

FraudBad counted it ourselves from official government data. The page shows the query and links to the data.

Never collected

Money the government is owed and hasn't collected, by its own count.

Fraud is not the same as an improper payment

An improper payment is money paid in the wrong amount, to the wrong person, or without the records to prove it was right. Some improper payments are fraud. Many are mistakes. A large share are payments where the paperwork simply isn't there.

That last kind is the reason this site exists. When a payment has no receipt, nobody can tell honest error from theft. The government's own estimate of fraud — $233 billion to $521 billion a year — is bigger than its yearly improper-payment total, because most fraud is never caught.

What we won't do

We won't name people who haven't been charged. We won't call a charge a conviction. We won't claim the RECEIPTS Act covers programs it doesn't: as drafted, it starts with federal orders over $25,000 placed through GSA's schedules.

Corrections

Found a number that's wrong or out of date? Send us the source. We fix errors and note the date.
